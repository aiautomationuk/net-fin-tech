Public Sector Equality Duty for Suppliers

28 November 2026

The Public Sector Equality Duty for suppliers matters in two ways. The duty itself sits on public bodies, such as councils and NHS trusts, but it also applies to any organisation carrying out public functions on their behalf. And because public bodies must show they have considered equality when they buy services, they often ask suppliers about it in tenders and contracts.

Quick answer: Under section 149 of the Equality Act 2010, public authorities must have due regard to equality when they carry out their functions. If your organisation carries out a public function, the duty applies to you in that work. Even where it does not, public buyers may ask for your equality policy, how you train staff and how you will help them meet the duty through the contract.

Key Takeaways

  • The Public Sector Equality Duty is set out in section 149 of the Equality Act 2010.

  • It applies to public authorities and to others when they carry out public functions.

  • The duty is to have due regard to three aims: tackling discrimination, advancing equality of opportunity and fostering good relations.

  • Public buyers often build equality requirements into tenders and contracts.

  • Suppliers who can show clear policies, training and data are better placed to answer those questions.

What the Duty Says

Section 149 of the Equality Act 2010 says a public authority must, in the exercise of its functions, have due regard to the need to:

  • eliminate discrimination, harassment, victimisation and other conduct the Act prohibits

  • advance equality of opportunity between people who share a relevant protected characteristic and people who do not

  • foster good relations between people who share a relevant protected characteristic and people who do not

The relevant protected characteristics listed in the section are age, disability, gender reassignment, pregnancy and maternity, race, religion or belief, sex and sexual orientation.

"Due regard" does not mean a particular result. It means the authority must think about equality properly, and at the right time, before it makes a decision. That is why many public bodies use Equality Impact Assessments to record their thinking.

When the Public Sector Equality Duty Applies to Suppliers

Section 149(2) says a person who is not a public authority, but who exercises public functions, must have due regard to the same matters when exercising those functions. In practice, this can include a private or voluntary sector organisation running a service that the public body would otherwise run itself.

Whether a particular contract involves a public function can be a legal question, so take advice if you are unsure. For many suppliers, such as a firm providing office cleaning or IT kit, the duty will not apply directly. The buyer still has to think about equality, though, and that shapes what it asks you, often alongside other prequalification checks such as SSIP accreditation for health and safety.

What Public Buyers Often Ask For

When public bodies tender for services, they commonly ask suppliers to show:

  • an equality, diversity and inclusion policy, and who is responsible for it

  • how staff are trained on equality and on the people they will serve

  • how complaints of discrimination or harassment are handled

  • how the service will be made accessible to disabled people and others with particular needs

  • what monitoring data you collect and how you will report it during the contract

The questions tend to be more detailed where the service is delivered directly to the public. If you are new to tendering, our guide to what to check before booking a health and safety adviser covers another area where buyers expect clear paperwork.

How to Prepare

Check Your Policies

Make sure your equality policy is current, signed off by someone senior and reflects how you actually work. A short, honest policy that staff know about is better than a long one nobody has read.

Train the People Who Deliver the Work

Buyers increasingly want to know that front-line staff, not just managers, understand equality law and the communities they serve. Keep records of who has been trained and when.

Collect Useful Data

Think about what you could report if asked, such as who uses your service, complaints and how they were resolved, and any adjustments you made. Agree what data the buyer needs before the contract starts, rather than trying to gather it afterwards.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the Public Sector Equality Duty apply to private companies?

Only when they are exercising public functions. Section 149(2) of the Equality Act 2010 extends the duty to those functions. Other private work is covered by the rest of the Act, not by this duty.

What does "due regard" mean?

It means giving proper, timely thought to the three aims of the duty before making a decision. It does not require a particular outcome. If you would like some help with this, get in touch with our team.

Is an Equality Impact Assessment a legal requirement?

The Act does not require a document with that name, but many public bodies use Equality Impact Assessments to show they have had due regard to the duty.

Does Fin-Tech UK provide equality training?

No. Fin-Tech UK is an independent guide. It explains what to check, but it does not provide training or consultancy, take bookings or endorse individual firms.

Suppliers who treat equality as part of how they deliver the work, rather than a box to tick at tender time, tend to find these questions much easier to answer.
