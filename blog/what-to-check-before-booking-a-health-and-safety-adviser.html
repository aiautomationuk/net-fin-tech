What to Check Before Booking a Health and Safety Adviser

21 November 2026

What to check before booking a health and safety adviser comes down to five things: their qualifications, their professional membership, experience in your kind of workplace, their insurance, and exactly what you will get for the fee. It is also worth knowing that the law does not require you to hire a consultant at all, so the first check is whether you need one.

Quick answer: Before booking a health and safety adviser, check their qualifications (such as a NEBOSH diploma), their membership grade with a body such as IOSH, whether they are on the OSHCR consultants register, their experience in your sector, their professional indemnity insurance, references, and a written scope of work with the cost.

Key Takeaways

  • Check qualifications and membership yourself. IOSH and OSHCR both let you look people up.

  • Sector experience matters. An adviser who knows offices may not know construction sites or kitchens.

  • Get the scope and cost in writing before any work starts.

  • You may not need an outside adviser. HSE says to use a competent person from your own workforce where you have one.

  • The legal duty stays with you as the employer, whoever gives the advice.

First, Do You Need an Adviser?

The Health and Safety Executive (HSE) says that, as an employer, you must appoint a competent person or people to help you meet your health and safety duties. It also says that if there is a competent person within your workforce, you should use them in preference to someone from outside. Many low-risk businesses, such as small offices and shops, can manage health and safety themselves.

Outside help makes sense when your business is larger, more complex or higher risk, or when nobody in-house has the knowledge. Even then, HSE is clear that managing health and safety remains your legal duty as the employer. An adviser helps you meet it; they do not take it over.

Health and Safety Adviser Checks: The Essential Criteria

1. Confirm Their Qualifications

Start with qualifications. NEBOSH (the National Examination Board in Occupational Safety and Health) awards the best-known ones, from the introductory National General Certificate up to diploma level. IOSH (the Institution of Occupational Safety and Health) is the main professional body, and its Chartered Member grade, CMIOSH, shows an adviser has both a higher-level qualification and assessed experience. Ask to see certificates, and check that the level matches the complexity of your workplace.

2. Check Professional Memberships and Registers

Membership of a professional body means an adviser has signed up to a code of conduct and to keeping their knowledge up to date. Besides IOSH, the main bodies are IIRSM (the International Institute of Risk and Safety Management) and, for occupational hygiene, BOHS (the British Occupational Hygiene Society). The Occupational Safety and Health Consultants Register (OSHCR) is a public register of consultants who hold a suitable membership grade with one of the recognised bodies, and you can search it by area and specialism. If you tender for work yourself, you may also come across SSIP, which our SSIP accreditation guide explains.

3. Ask About Experience in Your Sector

Qualifications show knowledge; experience shows whether it can be applied to a business like yours. Ask about recent work with organisations of a similar size and type. An adviser who mainly works with offices may not be the right choice for a warehouse, a commercial kitchen or a building site, where the risks are very different.

4. Verify Insurance and References

Ask for proof of professional indemnity insurance, which covers you if their advice turns out to be wrong, and public liability insurance for when they are on your premises. Then ask for two or three references from recent clients and speak to them. A good adviser will be happy to provide both. Our guide to the cost of business insurance in the UK explains these types of cover.

5. Test Their Knowledge of the Law

Ask how the adviser keeps their knowledge current and how they would apply the law to a business of your size. A good answer is specific and proportionate: it deals with the real risks in your workplace instead of producing a thick folder of paperwork. Be wary of anyone who cannot explain a requirement in plain English.

6. Confirm Scope of Services and Ongoing Support

Make sure you know exactly what is included. Some advisers offer a one-off risk assessment, while others provide ongoing support, staff training or help after an accident. Ask how often they would expect to review your arrangements, and what happens if you need help between visits.

How to Choose a Health and Safety Adviser

Clarify Your Business Needs

Before you start looking, be clear about what you need. Is it help with one specific issue, a review of the whole workplace, staff training, or a written policy? A manufacturing unit needs something very different from a small tech company, and knowing your needs makes it far easier to compare advisers.

Ask the Right Questions During Enquiry

These are the questions worth asking every adviser you speak to:

  • What qualifications and membership grade do you hold?

  • Can you provide references from businesses like mine?

  • How do you approach risk assessment, and how often do you recommend reviews?

  • Do you offer staff training and ongoing compliance support?

  • What are your fees, and what’s included in your service?

Asking everyone the same questions makes it much easier to compare their answers fairly.

Compare What You Get for the Fee

Fees vary with the size of your business, the risks involved and how much support you want, so ask each adviser for a written quote that sets out what is included. Check whether extra visits, training sessions or document updates are charged separately. For wider ideas on keeping costs under control, see our guide to reducing business overheads.

Assess Training and Compliance Support

If you need training, check whether the adviser delivers it in person or online, and whether it is tailored to your workplace or a standard course. Training only works if your team can relate it to the jobs they actually do.

Evaluate Adviser Communication and Style

Choose an adviser who communicates clearly. Ask to see a sample report: it should be practical, free of jargon and specific about what to do next. Recommendations you cannot understand will not get acted on. For more on this, read our article on Public Sector Equality Duty for suppliers.

What Your Health and Safety Adviser Should Deliver

Understanding the Legal Requirements

The main laws are the Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 and the Management of Health and Safety at Work Regulations 1999, along with rules specific to certain kinds of work. Your adviser should explain what these mean for you in plain English. Getting it wrong can lead to enforcement notices, fines or prosecution, and our article on what health and safety failures cost looks at the consequences.

Good Practice Beyond the Minimum

A good adviser helps you keep things working after the first visit. That means risk assessments that are reviewed when something changes, policies kept up to date, and incidents looked into properly. Ask how they would help you with this.

Monitoring and Reviewing Compliance

Health and safety is not a one-off job. Your adviser should suggest sensible review points, especially after a change to the workplace or an incident, and help you set up simple checks you can run yourself.

Supporting Staff and Management Training

Ask whether the adviser can deliver or arrange training for staff and managers, such as inductions and refresher sessions. It is also fair to ask what training they do themselves each year to stay current.

Red Flags and Common Pitfalls When Booking a Health and Safety Adviser

Warning Signs to Watch Out For

Be cautious of advisers who are reluctant to show proof of qualifications or insurance, give vague answers about the law, or quote a very low price without asking about your business. No references, badly written reports and a one-size-fits-all approach are all warning signs.

Risks of Inadequate Health and Safety Support

Poor advice can leave real risks unmanaged, which puts your staff in danger and leaves you exposed if an inspector visits or someone is hurt. It can also bury you in paperwork you do not need. Either way, the responsibility remains yours, which is why the checks above are worth the time.

How to Resolve Issues with Your Adviser

If you are unhappy with your adviser, raise your concerns in writing first. A good adviser will respond promptly. If that does not resolve it and the adviser belongs to a professional body, you can complain to that body. Keep copies of everything.

When to Review or Change Your Adviser

Review the arrangement once a year, and whenever your business grows or changes. If the advice is out of date or no longer fits how you work, it is reasonable to look for someone better suited.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I check before booking a health and safety adviser?

Check their qualifications, their membership grade with a professional body, their experience in your sector, their insurance and their references. Then get the scope of work and the cost in writing.

How often should I review my health and safety adviser’s credentials?

Once a year is sensible, and again after any major change to your business. Check that their membership and insurance are still current.

Are there official registers for health and safety consultants in the UK?

Yes. The Occupational Safety and Health Consultants Register (OSHCR) lists consultants who hold a suitable membership grade with a recognised professional body. You can search it online for free.

What should be included in a health and safety adviser’s service?

It varies, but common services are risk assessments, a review of your policies and procedures, staff training and ongoing support. Make sure the written agreement lists what is included and what costs extra.

What are the risks of hiring an unqualified health and safety adviser?

Poor advice can leave serious risks unmanaged or bury you in needless paperwork. As the employer you remain legally responsible, so the consequences of bad advice fall on you.

A few careful checks before you book will tell you whether an adviser is right for your business.
