SSIP Accreditation

14 November 2026

SSIP accreditation is usually worth it if you bid for construction, facilities or public sector work, because many buyers ask for it before they will even look at your price. If your customers are mainly households and nobody has asked for it, it is often a cost that can wait.

Quick answer: SSIP accreditation shows buyers that your health and safety arrangements have been checked by a recognised scheme. It pays for itself when it gets you through prequalification for contracts you can realistically win. Weigh the scheme fee, renewal and preparation time against the work it opens up.

Key Takeaways

  • SSIP stands for Safety Schemes in Procurement, a forum of health and safety assessment schemes.

  • Member schemes recognise each other, so you prove your competence once rather than for every buyer.

  • Principal contractors, councils and facilities firms often ask for it at the prequalification stage.

  • It is not a legal requirement, but it can decide whether you get onto a tender list.

  • Most of the work is getting your policy, risk assessments and records in order.

What SSIP Accreditation Is

SSIP stands for Safety Schemes in Procurement. It is an umbrella forum for the health and safety assessment schemes that buyers use when they vet contractors. Each member scheme checks a firm's health and safety arrangements against a shared set of core criteria, and the schemes recognise each other's results.

The aim is to cut duplication. Instead of filling in a different health and safety questionnaire for every buyer, you are assessed by one scheme and show the certificate. Without it, you may face a long questionnaire for each tender, or you may not get past the first round at all.

Who Benefits Most From SSIP Accreditation

Contractors and Subcontractors in Construction

Under the Construction (Design and Management) Regulations 2015, anyone appointing a contractor must make sure that contractor has the skills, knowledge and experience to work safely. An SSIP certificate gives principal contractors a simple way to show they checked. Trades who subcontract to larger firms often find it is expected before they are added to an approved supplier list.

Firms Bidding for Public Sector and Facilities Work

Councils, schools, housing associations and facilities management firms buying maintenance, cleaning or grounds work often use prequalification questionnaires. Many construction questionnaires are based on PAS 91, a British Standards Institution specification, and SSIP certification is commonly accepted for the health and safety section.

When It Can Wait

A firm that works mainly for homeowners, or whose customers never ask for it, may see little return for now. Look back through your recent tender documents and supplier forms. If none of them mention SSIP or a member scheme, put the money into winning work first.

Weighing the Cost Against the Work It Wins

Treat SSIP like any other overhead: compare what you spend with the work it opens up. There is no single price, because the cost depends on a few things.

  • The scheme fee, which usually depends on the scheme you choose and the size of your firm.

  • Renewal, as most schemes reassess each year, so budget for it annually.

  • Your time, gathering policies, records and evidence before you apply.

  • Outside advice, if you pay a consultant to write or review documents.

Then look at the contracts you could bid for. One regular subcontract or a place on a framework can cover the cost many times over, but only if you are realistically in the running on price and capacity. What You Need Before You Apply

Schemes look for evidence that your arrangements are real and used on site, not just documents bought off the shelf. Most will ask to see the following.

  • A health and safety policy, which the Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 requires in writing once you employ five or more people.

  • Risk assessments, with significant findings recorded if you employ five or more people.

  • Method statements for higher-risk tasks.

  • Access to competent health and safety advice, in-house or from outside.

  • Training records and relevant qualifications for your staff.

  • Your approach to reporting accidents under RIDDOR.

  • An employers' liability insurance certificate if you employ staff.

Good record keeping elsewhere in the business helps. Firms getting ready for the Making Tax Digital changes often find it a sensible moment to tidy all their compliance paperwork at once.

Choosing a Scheme

Start with what your buyers ask for. Some contractors name a particular scheme in their supplier forms, while others accept any SSIP member. Thanks to mutual recognition, getting a second SSIP-recognised certificate usually involves less paperwork once you hold the first, although each scheme still charges its own fee. The SSIP Forum publishes the current list of member schemes on its website.

Getting Help With the Application

Plenty of firms complete the application themselves. Others bring in an adviser to write or review the policy, risk assessments and method statements, which can save time if your records are thin or you have failed an assessment before. If you are preparing a first application in Essex or nearby, a health and safety consultancy such as SafeAid UK can help get your documents ready.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does SSIP stand for?

SSIP stands for Safety Schemes in Procurement. It is a forum of health and safety assessment schemes that recognise each other's results.

Is SSIP accreditation a legal requirement?

No. The law does not require it. Buyers ask for it as an easy way to check your health and safety competence, which construction clients and contractors must do when they appoint someone.

How long does SSIP accreditation last?

Most member schemes reassess firms each year, so plan for an annual renewal. Check the terms of the scheme you choose.

Do I need SSIP if I only work for homeowners?

Usually not. It mainly matters when a contractor, council or facilities firm asks for it as part of their supplier checks.
